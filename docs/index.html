ClearBudget

A private desktop budget planner built around a question, not a ledger: will the month survive? It models forward solvency, bill timing and credit card pressure before problems arrive.

Download View source

Free and open source. Local-first. Multi-user. Your finances never leave your machine.

Most budgeting apps are retrospective ledgers that tell you where the money went. ClearBudget is forward-looking: it projects solvency for the months ahead and warns about mid-month overdrafts before they happen.

What ClearBudget does

Most budgeting software is retrospective. ClearBudget looks forward instead.

Safe to Spend Today

One actionable number at the top of the Solvency tab's Safe to Spend page: the most you could spend today while every month that still stands on its own keeps standing, with the reach of that promise and the day that limits it both named. A month already under your buffer with nothing spent is a shortfall rather than a limit on today, so it does not silence the figure; it is named on its own line, in red, with the amount and the fact that spending the headline deepens it. The line above it stays muted, because that one is a caution about a figure you can still act on while the red one reports a gap no restraint closes. The buffer (twenty by default, zero if you plan to the wire) and how far ahead to look (one to twelve months, four by default) are yours to set.

It sits on that page rather than beside your entered balances, because it is a promise about months that have not happened rather than a fact about the account. It counts the income this month has as arriving again in every later month with no entry of that name. The page states that assumption directly beneath the number.

Today is often the tightest day of the month, so beneath it a short schedule gives the figure from each later day money lands, each line naming the day still holding it down. That day is frequently in a later month, which is the point of it: a bigger balance later this month does not mean more to spend when next month has to survive too.

Forward solvency

Projects the next two months and answers the only operational question that matters: will the balance hold?

What the month needs

Every month states the gap between its full bills and its full income, so a month that does not hold together comes with a size rather than just a warning. A month can close in credit while still running at a loss, which is exactly what a closing balance alone hides. Credit card interest is reported beside that figure and never inside it, because it accrues on the cards rather than leaving the bank account.

If the months ahead are like this one

Months you have not filled in yet look poorer than they are, so a reading that counts only what is typed reports a shortfall you do not have. A Safe to Spend page, reached by a button that names that figure, assumes the income entered for this month arrives again in every later month with no entry of that name. It fills gaps only, never showing a month as worse than what you entered. It states that assumption in words rather than leaving you to infer it, then names exactly what has to arrive and by when.

It then walks the next two months again on that assumption, which is usually why you opened it. When the bank page says a month goes overdrawn on a given day with no facility, the question you have is whether the money you expect would rescue it. The page says outright whether the month holds, where it opens and closes and which income rescues it on which day. It also carries Safe to Spend Today, because that figure rests on this page's assumption rather than on money already in the account.

Mid-month overdraft alerts

Catches the dip when bills cluster before payday, even when the month still ends positive; it also names the first month you would go overdrawn.

Credit card pressure

Per-card utilisation, interest and a six-month balance projection, colour-coded by available headroom. Credit-limit changes can be scheduled ahead so every projection honours them.

Bill and income timing

Per-month skips, amount and due-day overrides, paid and received flags and one-off entries. Bills and income alike carry a final month, so something that has stopped names when it stopped instead of being erased. Deleting either offers the same choice: stop it from the month you are viewing; delete it entirely. A bill can also change amount from a month onward, for a rent increase, without rewriting what earlier months cost.

Self-maintaining balance

Set your bank balance once: dated bills and income are applied to it at local midnight on their day and ticked off automatically. Delete an applied item and the amount is handed back.

A Graph tab

The viewed month plotted as a page of its own, the fifth tab in the tray, stepped between months by the same arrows every tab shares. A switch on the page chooses what is plotted, the bank balance day by day or every credit card on one chart; a pilot button swaps bar and line renderings. Each day's bar is read against the overdraft you have arranged: its calm resting colour at or above zero, amber inside your facility, red only where a payment would actually bounce.

Shareable reports

Export the month as a single self-contained web page carrying both the bar and line renderings; export a range of months as a folder: an index charting month ends against the lowest point reached inside each month, with a table that adds up (opening balance, income, bills, net, closing balance, the low and its day) and every row leading to that month's own day-by-day page. Nothing is ever fetched from anywhere, so a file can be emailed and a folder can be copied to a stick and opened offline.

Several budgets, one sign-in

Keep more than one budget under a single account, each with its own bills, income, credit cards, overrides and settings. A household beside a business; this year beside a plan for next. Create one from the File menu, then switch, rename and delete from the navigation tray. Switching changes nothing in either budget; the one you already have keeps the file it always used, with no upgrade step to run.

Back up everything at once

Saving a budget covers that budget. Backing up everything covers the lot: every account and every one of their budgets in a single zip, restorable in full onto a new or rebuilt machine. A restore is checked in a staging area first, so a file that is not a genuine backup changes nothing. The zip is not encrypted, holding password hashes rather than passwords; the app says so before it writes one.

Private by design

Per-user isolated databases with a bcrypt sign-in. A Remember me option keeps your credentials in the operating system's own credential store, never in a plain file. No cloud, no account servers. The one network request the app ever makes is a daily check of the project's GitHub releases for a newer version, carrying nothing about you or your budget; when one is published it offers the download for your platform. One click saves the whole budget to a single file you choose and loads it back later; the app remembers where, separately for every account.

Light and dark

One button switches the whole application, sign-in screen included and the choice is remembered. The graphs follow, with a palette chosen to stay readable on each background.

Usable without a mouse

An explicit keyboard path through every screen and dialog: Tab and the arrows step the same ring, the tabs are walked one at a time, long pages scroll from the keyboard and what has focus is always ringed.

25 currencies

Choose your currency once and every figure follows it. Defaults to GBP.

Installs without touching your data

A native package per platform: a per-user Windows setup needing no administrator rights, a macOS disk image and a Linux Flatpak. Installing, upgrading, repairing and uninstalling all deal in program files only, so your budgets, accounts and saved theme survive every one of them.

Five views, one question

Each tab is a different angle on the same question: whether this month holds together and whether the ones after it do. Each carries a picture rather than a name, sitting in the navigation tray beside the other controls and naming itself on hover.

Monthly Budget

Bills and income for the month, with a balance that keeps itself up to date as dated items fall due, the projected month-end figure for future months and a mid-month overdraft warning.

Solvency

Two pages, each a whole answer rather than half of one long scroll. The bank page is built only from money you have entered: your account position, then the next two months walked day by day. The Safe to Spend page adds the one assumption worth making, that the months ahead resemble this one, states it in words and shows what follows from it.

Credit Cards

One panel per card with live pro-rated balances and a six-month projection strip across every card.

Graph

The viewed month as a chart: the bank balance day by day or every card on one line chart, switched on the page itself, with the HTML exports beside it.

Archive

Completed months summarised by year, with drill-down into any individual month.

ClearBudget is a free, private, local-first desktop budget planner for Windows, macOS and Linux. It leads with a Safe to Spend Today figure: the most you could spend today while every month that still stands on its own keeps standing, named with the day that constrains it, followed by what you could spend from each later day of the month as income lands. It models forward solvency for the next two months, detects mid-month overdrafts when bills cluster before income, tracks credit card utilisation and interest with a six-month balance projection and supports multiple users with bcrypt sign-in and isolated per-user databases, each holding any number of named budgets. A Graph tab plots the month as the bank balance day by day or every card on one chart; a month exports as a self-contained web page and a range of months exports as a self-contained folder with an index. It has light and dark themes and a full keyboard path through every screen and dialog. Built with Python and PySide6, it runs offline with no cloud dependency (its only network request is a daily check of the project's GitHub releases for a newer version) and is open source under the LGPL-3.0 licence.
